2 · What is artificial intelligence? (4 min)

Artificial intelligence is the broad field: machines doing tasks that would need intelligence.

Machine learning is the part that learns rules from examples instead of being given the rules.

Deep learning is one kind of machine learning, with many-layered neural networks.

On the screen: a rule a person wrote, and a rule learned from real flowers. Which one would you trust, and why?

If the rule is learned, how does the learning actually happen?

3 · What is machine learning? (3 min)

Traditional programming: we write the rules. Machine learning: we give examples with answers, and the algorithm finds the rule. That is training.

Inference is using the trained rule on a new case.

The score on flowers it has already seen is optimistic. The honest score is on flowers it has never seen.

Where does training sit in a whole project, from raw data to a prediction we can check?

4 · How learning works (4 min)

Seven stages, one dataset: 150 iris flowers. Collect, prepare, choose features, split, train, predict, evaluate.

We split first. The test flowers are locked away before we choose features or train, so the test stays a fair check.

Two rows have identical measurements and labels. For this demonstration we keep one; in a real study we would check whether they are repeated records or two different flowers.

Training accuracy 98.1 % · test accuracy 91.1 % on the unseen flowers.

Feature scores are computed on the training flowers only. The tree depth is chosen by cross-validation on the training data.

The book groups this work into three steps. What are they, and what can a model learn without labels?

5 · §8.2 Three steps in the machine-learning process (6 min)

Step 1, ingest the data. Step 2, analyze it with a method. Step 3, produce results that someone uses.

Supervised learning has the answers in the training data, the labels. Unsupervised learning has no labels and looks for structure.

Here are the same flowers. With labels we see species. Without labels, K-Means finds three groups. The groups are patterns, not species names.

Colours: species are teal / green / violet; clusters are pink / amber / blue. Short of time? Skip the Tesla case and the tidy-data detail.

We know where learning happens. What kind of answer are we asking the model to produce?

6 · §8.3 Characteristics of machine-learning methods (13 min)

Three questions describe any method. Is the answer a category or a number — classification or regression? Do we want to understand the relationship, inference, or just predict well? And how interpretable is the model versus how complex?

A decision tree asks one question at a time. Here is the book’s car seat tree: read it from the top, and follow one store to its prediction.

Careful with explanatory variables: a variable that is just another version of the answer is a surrogate and makes the model look better than it is.

Deeper trees fit more detail but are harder to explain.

A category answers which kind; a numerical model answers how much. How does a model learn to predict a number?

7 · Worked example: Can money buy a better movie rating? (12 min)

Source: Shah, Hands-On Problem 8.4, p. 233 — not a DS4A dataset

Pick one of these three movies; we will follow it into the next section. Now predict: will adding social-media reactions to budget make ratings much easier to predict?

Which single factor first? Our rule: the strongest correlation with the rating. That is budget. It is an association, not a cause.

For one movie: the line predicts this, the real rating is that, the difference is the error. We square it so positive and negative errors don’t cancel and big errors count more.

The cost J adds up all the squared errors. Gradient descent takes small steps downhill on that cost until it stops improving.

The slope of the line is not the gradient. The gradient is the direction in which the cost rises fastest; each step goes the other way.

Bigger steps? With α = 2.2 the steps overshoot and the cost grows.

Budget alone R² = 0.104 (about 10 % of the variation) · with likes, dislikes and comments R² = 0.219 · descent with α = 0.1 settles at cost J = 0.447. All measured on the same movies the model was fitted to.

Say “in-sample”: these R² values say nothing yet about new movies.

Practise here: the same model in 3D; Problem 8.4 activity; Restaurants (Problem 8.1); Airlines; Rock.

We reduced error on these examples. What tells us the model will work on new ones?

8 · §8.4 Machine-learning method evaluation (12 min)

Judge a model on data it did not learn from. The book splits 400 car-seat stores into 319 for training and 81 for testing.

Two kinds of mistakes: a false positive and a false negative. In medicine, which is worse depends on the situation.

Accuracy, precision, recall and specificity each answer a different question about those mistakes.

To choose a model, use validation or cross-validation. Keep the test set for one final check.

Back to our movies: on movies the model never saw, the budget model does about as well as guessing the average.

Confusion matrix (Table 8.3): TP 22, FP 11, FN 11, TN 37 · accuracy 0.73 · precision 0.67 · recall 0.67 · specificity 0.77 · car seats: cross-validation picks depth 4, test 67.9 % · movies: budget R² 0.104 fitted vs −0.003 held-out; median 0.067 over 50 splits.

Specificity = TN / (TN + FP). The book prints TN / (TN + FN); in its example FP = FN, so the number is the same.

We supplied measurements to the earlier models. What if the input is the image itself?

9 · §8.5 Deep learning (6 min)

An image is just numbers: this handwritten digit is 8 × 8 = 64 shades.

A neural network passes those numbers through layers and learns its own features from the pixels.

Here is one digit it gets right, and one it gets wrong.

The cost: much more data, computing power, and it is hard to explain. And it is not automatically the best method — on ordinary tables of data, tree models often do as well or better.

Say “can”, not “always”: deep learning can learn useful representations; how well depends on the task, the data and the model.

Is the far end of the trade-off always necessary? What does recent research say?

10 · Recent research: accuracy versus interpretability (3 min)

In §8.3 we saw a trade-off: flexible models may predict better but are harder to explain. A 2025 study tested whether that is really true.

Kruschel and colleagues compared 14 models on 20 real tabular datasets, with cross-validation and tuning: 68,500 model runs. Seven were modern interpretable models called GAMs: like linear regression, but each variable gets a curve you can plot and read.

The result: an interpretable model, EBM, came second of 14, ahead of XGBoost, random forest and neural networks; only CatBoost was better. On classification the gap to the best black box was at most 0.015 AUROC.

But the old interpretable models, a straight line and a single tree, ranked near the bottom. So the trade-off is real for them, not for modern interpretable models.

What it means for us: on tables of data, try an interpretable model first; reading the model also helps us find bias. The limits: tabular data only. Noé's scan is an image, so this result does not carry over directly.

Average rank after tuning (1 = best): CatBoost 2.85 · EBM 4.05 · XGBoost 4.30 · random forest 4.83 · MLP 6.48 · linear/logistic regression 11.08 · decision tree 11.55. EBM is first on the 10 classification datasets (3.35). Black boxes won on 14 of 20 datasets.

These are the paper's published numbers (Table 4), not computed by our app. Say "on tabular data" every time: the authors themselves say images and text may differ. Business & Information Systems Engineering 68(1), 159–183, online 13 Feb 2025, doi:10.1007/s12599-024-00922-2.

A powerful model still learns from a particular sample of the world. What makes that sample good enough?

11 · §8.6 Use high-quality data (5 min)

Garbage in, garbage out. Four dimensions: freshness, volume, diversity, quality.

Fresh: the world changes, a new scanner changes the images. Volume: more examples help, up to a point. Diversity: a model can’t recognise what it never saw. Quality: wrong labels and blurred data cost accuracy.

Training data should represent the cases the model will meet.

Freshness demo: 97.5 % drops to 90.0 % with the new scanner; retraining restores it.

Who is missing from the sample, and who pays for the model’s mistakes?

12 · Ethics in practice: social justice in data science (4 min)

Bias enters through the data, the people who build systems, and how systems are used.

Facial analysis trained mostly on light-skinned faces fails other people more often.

Here is the mechanism, measured on penguins: a group that is rare in the training data gets far more errors, while overall accuracy still looks fine.

AI can also reduce human bias — if it is checked for each group and people stay accountable.

Gender Shades (2018): up to 34.7 % errors for darker-skinned women vs at most 0.8 % for lighter-skinned men · penguin demo: 27 % error for the rare group while overall accuracy is 94.6 %; after mitigation 3.5 %.

Gender Shades measured commercial gender classification, not police identity matching. The penguins are an analogy, not face data.

Back to Noé: what evidence would we need before trusting the scan’s prediction?

13 · Conclusion: back to Noé’s scan (3 min)

An impressive prediction is not yet evidence.

A prediction is a score for one scan. A decision is what happens to the patient — and people make it and own it.

Before trusting a model, ask for five things: representative data, suitable validation, known error costs, performance for each group, and human review.

Three recall questions: let the class discuss each before → checks. Then the sources slide: that is the end of the presentation.
