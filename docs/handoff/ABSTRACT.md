# Lecture 8 — Machine Learning: what evidence makes a prediction trustworthy?

*Introduction to Data Science, 2026/27 · Data Science for All, Chapter 8, with Shah, A Hands-On Introduction to Data Science, Chapter 8*

A short film follows Noé, who helps radiologists review scans while a system called RadiologyML offers a prediction for
every image. The lecture then works through DS4A Chapter 8 in order, asking one question throughout: what evidence would
make us trust a model's prediction enough to use it? Students see machine learning as learning rules from examples, the
three steps of a machine-learning process, the characteristics that distinguish methods, a worked regression example on real
movies (gradient descent, squared error, learning rate), and the evaluation that separates a good fit from a good
prediction: validation, test sets, confusion matrices and the costs of different mistakes. Deep learning, the quality of
training data and the chapter's ethics box show where models fail and who is affected. The lecture ends back at the scan,
with a checklist of the evidence still missing. Every number on screen is computed live from the books' datasets.

## Learning objectives
By the end, students can:
1. distinguish AI, machine learning and deep learning, and say which part of a system is learned from examples;
2. describe the three steps of the machine-learning process, and the difference between supervised and unsupervised learning;
3. classify a problem as regression or classification, and as inference or prediction, and explain the trade-off between
   interpretability and complexity;
4. explain how gradient descent reduces a squared-error cost, and what the learning rate changes;
5. explain why a model must be judged on data it did not learn from, distinguish validation from a final test, and compute
   accuracy, precision, recall and specificity from a confusion matrix;
6. explain why in-sample R² is not evidence of predictive performance;
7. name the data-quality dimensions (freshness, volume, diversity, quality, representativeness) and how unequal errors arise
   for under-represented groups;
8. list the evidence needed before a prediction informs a decision: representative data, suitable validation, known error
   costs, subgroup performance and human review.

Format: about 80 minutes (planned; to be confirmed in rehearsal), presenter-paced, with optional in-class activities.
